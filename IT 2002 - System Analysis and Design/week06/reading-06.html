01Introduction

Reading 5 showed how to draw a use-case diagram. The diagram tells us who uses the system and what the system does for them. But it is a black box: it shows the use case Manage Appointments, and it does not say what happens inside. How does a receptionist make an appointment? What if the patient is new? What if the patient wants to cancel?

This reading opens the black box in two steps. First we draw an activity diagram: a picture of the steps of one use case, in order, with the decisions and the parallel work. Then we write a use-case description: a short document in plain language that lists the same steps as a normal flow, subflows and alternate flows. At the end we check that the use-case diagram, the activity diagram and the description tell the same story. This check is called verification and validation.

The reading follows Lecture 6 and the second half of Chapter 3 of the textbook (Tegarden et al., 7th edition). Sections 3 to 7 are about activity diagrams, with worked examples. Sections 8 to 11 are about use-case descriptions, with full examples. Section 12 is about verification and validation. Section 13 lists the most common mistakes.

After completing this reading, you should be able to:

  • tell a decision from a fork, and a merge from a join;
  • find black-hole and miracle activities and repair them;
  • follow the five steps that create an activity diagram;
  • explain the four types of use cases: overview, detail, essential and real;
  • write the overview section and the flows of events of a use-case description;
  • follow the seven guidelines for writing steps; and
  • verify and validate the three functional models with a walkthrough and a consistency check.

03Modeling a business process

3.1 Where activity diagrams fit

A business process is a set of activities that together reach a goal for a customer: for example, to rent a video, to borrow a book or to book a doctor's appointment. A process often crosses several people, departments and objects, so it is easy to forget a step. An activity diagram shows the activities of a process and the order in which they happen. It is a graphical model, and users can read it with very little training. For this reason it is a strong tool for showing the users what we understood.

An activity diagram can model any process: a high-level business workflow, the details of one use case, or even one method of a program. In Chapter 3 of the textbook we draw one activity diagram for each use case. The use-case diagram gives the list of use cases, the activity diagram opens each of them, and the use-case description writes it down in words (Figure 3.1).

3.2 Seven best practices

Process modeling is a skill that grows with practice. The seven practices below help from the first diagram. The list comes from M. Schedlbauer's book on business process modeling, which the textbook and the lecture quote.

Table 3.1 · Seven best practices for process modeling
#PracticeWhat it means
1Be realisticYou cannot identify everything, and not everything is equally important.
2Be agileWhat you do identify, identify rigorously, and then move on.
3Work in teamsModeling alone leads to missed or mixed-up tasks. Other eyes find errors.
4Whiteboard firstUse a CASE tool only to document the process after you understand it.
5IterateRevise the model every time you learn something new.
6Stay focusedWrite other processes that you discover on a to-do list; do not draw them now.
7AbstractLeave out minor tasks. See the forest, not one tree.

04Elements of an activity diagram

Figure 4.1 shows the symbols we use. The textbook shows the full syntax in its Figure 3-6; our figure is a simplified drawing of the same elements.

4.1 Actions and activities

An action or an activity is something that is done for a business reason. It can be manual (the receptionist writes the name) or computerized (the system prints the receipt). It is drawn as a rounded rectangle. The only difference between the two words: an activity can be broken down into smaller activities or actions, and an action is a simple step that cannot be broken down. Business diagrams usually use only activities.

The name starts with a verb and ends with a noun, for example Get Patient Information or Make Payment Arrangements. The name must be short, but the reader must understand what is done.

Weak names
“Patient”, “Payment” (a noun alone does not say what is done) · “Process”, “Do the check” (too vague) · “The system saves the record to the database table” (too long, too technical)
Better names
Get Patient Information · Make Payment Arrangements · Check Overdue Videos · Update Records

4.2 Object nodes and object flows

Activities change or create objects: a form, an order, a patient record. An object node shows such an object in the diagram. It is drawn as a rectangle with the name of the class of the object inside. An object flow is a dashed arrow between an activity and an object node; it shows that the object goes into or comes out of the activity.

In most business diagrams we leave out object nodes and object flows. We keep them in one situation: when an activity collects information that another activity needs later, and not directly after. In the appointment example, Get Patient Information finds out whether the patient is new or old; two steps later the diagram must use this fact. The textbook shows object nodes in Figure 3-7 for demonstration.

4.3 Control flows and control nodes

A control flow is a solid arrow. It shows the order of execution: which activity comes next. Control flows connect only activities and nodes, and never object nodes. Control nodes are the small symbols that start, stop, split and join the flows. There are seven of them: initial, final-activity, final-flow, decision, merge, fork and join. The first three start and stop a process.

Table 4.1 · The nodes that start and stop a process
NodeSymbolMeaning
Initial nodeA small filled circleThe process starts here. Use only one, at the top or top left.
Final-activity nodeA circle around a filled circle (bull's-eye)The whole process stops at once, even if some activities are not finished. Use one, at the bottom or bottom right.
Final-flow nodeA small circle with an XOnly one path stops; the other parallel paths go on. Avoid it if you can: many exits make a process hard to understand.
RememberAim for one way in and one way out: a single initial node and a single final-activity node.

4.4 Decision and merge

A decision node is a diamond. It shows a test, and one of the paths that leave the diamond is taken (either / or). Each path has a guard condition in square brackets or as a short label, for example [new patient] and [old patient]. The guards must be mutually exclusive (two guards cannot be true together) and complete (every possible value has a path). A merge node is also a diamond; it brings the paths that were split by a decision back together. Sometimes a merge is not needed and the paths can go directly to the next activity. Use common sense.

4.5 Fork and join

A fork node is a thick bar. It splits one flow into several flows that run in parallel: all of them are taken (and / and). A join node is also a thick bar. It brings the parallel flows back into one flow and waits for all of them. Figure 4.2 puts a decision and a fork side by side.

Table 4.2 · Decision and fork compared
Decision and mergeFork and join
SymbolDiamondThick bar
PathsOne path is taken (either / or)All paths are taken (and / and)
LabelsGuard condition on every pathNo guards
ExamplePay by card or by cashPack the items and print the invoice at the same time
Closes withMergeJoin (waits for all paths)

4.6 Swimlanes

An activity diagram can be drawn without saying who does each activity. When we want to show responsibility, we divide the diagram into swimlanes, vertical or horizontal. Each lane belongs to one role, department or system, and each activity is drawn in the lane of the one who performs it. Swimlanes are very useful for business workflows:

  • they show the responsibility;
  • they separate external roles, internal roles and the system;
  • they make handovers visible (an arrow that crosses from one lane to another);
  • they help later, when we write the use-case description.

05Guidelines & common mistakes

5.1 Guidelines

The textbook follows the guidelines of Scott Ambler. Table 5.1 groups them into what to do before you draw and what to do while you draw.

Table 5.1 · Guidelines for activity diagrams
WhenGuideline
Before you drawSet the scope (the context) of the process and give the diagram a title.
Identify the activities, the control flows and the object flows.
Identify the decisions that are part of the process.
Look for parallel work (forks and joins).
When you drawUse one initial node (top or top left) and, in most cases, one final-activity node (bottom or bottom right).
Include only the important decisions and forks in a high-level diagram.
Make the guard conditions mutually exclusive and complete.
Lay out the flow from left to right or top to bottom, with as few crossing lines as possible.

5.2 Black-hole and miracle activities

Two mistakes are easy to find if you look for them. A black-hole activity has inflows but no outflow: the process enters it and never leaves. A miracle activity has outflows but no inflow: it happens from nowhere, as if by a miracle. In Figure 5.1, Print Receipt is a black hole and Update Stock is a miracle. The repair is to add the missing arrow: from the activity to the next activity or to the final node, or from the initial node or another activity into the miracle activity.

CheckDoes every activity have at least one arrow going in and one arrow going out? This one question finds both mistakes.

5.3 More things to check

  • Does the diagram have one initial node? Two starting points confuse the reader.
  • Does every decision have a guard on every outgoing path?
  • Are all names verb + noun, and short?
  • Is every fork closed by a join, and every decision (when needed) by a merge?
  • Does the diagram fit on one page? If not, the process is too big: split it.

06Creating an activity diagram

The textbook gives five steps. You can use them for any process. Here is each step for the use case Manage Appointments of a doctor's office.

  1. Choose a process. Start with a use case from the use-case diagram. Review the requirements definition, the interview notes and other documents.
  2. Identify the activities. List what must be done: Request Appointment, Get Patient Information, Update Patient Information, Create New Patient, Make Payment Arrangements, Create Appointment, Change Appointment, Cancel Appointment.
  3. Identify the control flows and nodes. The use-case diagram has extend relationships: Make Payment Arrangements and Update Patient Information happen only sometimes. Every extend suggests a decision node.
  4. Identify the object flows and nodes. Needed only if information from one activity is used later. Here the fact “old or new patient” is captured early in Get Patient Information.
  5. Lay out and draw. Put the nodes in order, avoid crossing lines, and draw the diagram on a whiteboard first.
Useful hintEvery <<extend>> on the use-case diagram suggests a decision node in the activity diagram. Capture the information that the decision needs as early as possible, for example in Get Patient Information.

07Worked examples of activity diagrams

The best way to learn a diagram is to look at many diagrams. This section has three worked examples and a table that shows how to read a story and find the symbols.

7.1 Manage Appointments

A simplified version of Figure 3-7 of the textbook. The process starts when a patient asks for an appointment. The receptionist gets the patient's information. A decision follows: a new patient is created first, and an old patient gets an update of the information. The two paths meet in a merge. Then payment arrangements are made, and the appointment is created. There is one initial node and one final-activity node, and every activity has an arrow in and an arrow out. The textbook diagram also includes Change Appointment and Cancel Appointment for old patients; we leave them out to keep the picture small.

7.2 Order Processing

An order has two jobs that do not depend on each other: the goods must be filled and shipped, and the customer must be invoiced and must pay. Two people can do them at the same time, so we use a fork. After both jobs are finished, the join lets the process go on and the order is closed. The textbook shows a similar diagram in Figure 3-8.

RememberThe join waits. If the payment never arrives, the order is never closed.

7.3 Borrow Resource (Library Management System)

This example comes from the Library Management System of the textbook (Figure 3-9). A patron brings a resource and an ID card. The librarian checks the card, then checks overdue resources and unpaid fees. If something is wrong, the checkout is denied. Otherwise the librarian gets the resource. What happens next depends on the resource: a physical resource that is available is checked out; if it is not available and the library owns it, it is reserved. (The textbook also covers online resources and borrowing from another library; our figure leaves these out.)

Our picture is simplified. It has several decisions and several exits that lead to the same final node. Deny Checkout receives two arrows, one from each failed check. This is correct: an activity may have more than one inflow. When Susan, the analyst in the textbook, drew this diagram, she found that the requirements were not complete, and she went back to change them. This is normal: drawing a process often shows gaps in the requirements.

08Use-case descriptions

8.1 Why we need them

A use-case diagram gives a bird's-eye view. An activity diagram opens the black box in a graphical way. A use-case description documents everything about one use case in a form that is easy for the users to read. It is based on the functional requirements, the use-case diagram and the activity diagram. It is also the document from which we find the classes and operations in the next chapters, and later the test cases and the screens. So it must be written with care, and the project team must work closely with the users.

Rule from Reading 5A use case belongs to one role, the primary actor, and not to a person. A receptionist plays many roles (makes appointments, answers the telephone, files records); each role is a different primary actor.

8.2 Four types of use cases

We classify use cases on two dimensions: the amount of information (overview or detail) and the purpose (essential or real).

Table 8.1 · Types of use cases
TypeMeaningWhen
OverviewBasic information only; the user and the analyst agree on the high-level requirements. It goes with the use-case diagram.Very early in the project.
DetailAll the information needed to document the use case. It is based on the activity diagram.After the overview is agreed.
EssentialOnly what is needed to understand the functionality. Independent of the implementation.Analysis (this course).
RealSpecific steps of using the implemented system, e.g. “look the date up in Google Calendar”.Design, implementation and testing.

Example: an essential use case says that the receptionist matches the patient's wishes with the available times. A real use case says that the receptionist looks the dates up in Google Calendar. In this course we write detail, essential use cases. We document overview use cases with the use-case diagram and detail use cases with the activity diagram and the description.

8.3 The overview section

A use-case description has two parts. The overview section identifies the use case and gives background information. The detail section lists the flows of events (Figure 8.1).

Table 8.2 · Elements of the overview section
ElementWhat goes in it
Name and IDA verb–noun name and a unique number. The number lets the team trace design decisions back to the requirement.
TypeOverview or detail; essential or real.
Primary actorThe role that starts the use case. The use case meets the goal of this actor.
Brief descriptionOne sentence with the essence of the use case.
Importance levelHigh, medium or low; used to prioritize. A more formal way (Larman) rates each use case from 0 to 5 on criteria such as business value, risk and complexity.
Stakeholders and interestsEveryone who has an interest in the use case, and what they want. The list always includes the primary actor.
TriggerThe event that starts the use case. External (a customer places an order, a fire alarm rings) or temporal (a book is overdue, the rent must be paid).
RelationshipsAssociation (the actors), include, extend and generalization (the other use cases).

8.4 Relationships

The relationships of the description are the same ones you drew in Reading 5.

AssociationCommunication between the use case and an actor (in the sample: Patient).
IncludeA mandatory part that is a separate use case, so that a complex use case can be broken into simpler ones.
ExtendOptional behavior, executed only if a condition is met (in the sample: Create New Patient, Make Payment Arrangements, Update Patient Information).
GeneralizationInheritance: the specialized use case inherits the common behavior.

The sample has no include and no generalization, and the description leaves these two lines empty (“none”).

8.5 The detail section: flows of events

The essence of a use case is its flow of events. The detail section has three kinds of flows.

Normal flowThe steps that normally happen, in order. The typical successful path.
SubflowsParts of the normal flow written separately to keep it simple. A step calls a subflow, e.g. “Call S-1: Create New Patient Subflow”. A subflow could become an include use case if it makes sense by itself.
Alternate / exceptional flowsThings that do happen but are not the norm. Each is labeled with the number of the step it replaces: 3a is an alternative to step 3. Pass means “do nothing”. An alternate flow could become an extend use case.

Which flow is normal and which is alternate is often a free choice. In the example, creating a new patient is the normal flow and the old patient is the alternate flow (3a). Steps 3 and 3a could be swapped without changing the use case.

8.6 How much to factor out

When should steps move into a subflow? When should a subflow become a separate use case? The answer is the complexity: the harder the use case is to understand, the more you factor out. But there is a price: more use cases make the use-case diagram more cluttered. You choose between a more complex diagram with simple descriptions and a simpler diagram with complex descriptions. There is no single right answer; it depends on the analyst, the client and the problem. The optional characteristics (complexity, estimated time, data flows, constraints, preconditions and postconditions) can be added if the project needs them. The sample in the textbook is the minimum.

09Writing good steps

Writing the flow of events well is a skill that grows with experience. The textbook gives seven guidelines.

Table 9.1 · Seven guidelines for writing the steps of a flow of events
#GuidelineWeak stepBetter step
1Use SVDPI sentences: subject, verb, direct object, (preposition, indirect object).The appointment is made.The Receptionist creates the Appointment for the Patient.
2Make the initiator and the receiver clear. The initiator is the subject, the receiver is the direct object.Information is given.The Patient provides the Receptionist with their information.
3Write as an independent observer, with a bird's-eye view.I ask the patient for the name.The Receptionist asks the Patient for the name.
4Keep all steps at the same level of abstraction.Take the fork out of the drawer. Prepare the cake.Set the table. Prepare the cake.
5Describe a sensible transaction: request, validate, process, result.The system saves the order.The Customer sends the order. The System validates it. The System stores it. The System shows the result.
6KISS: keep it simple. If the use case is too long or too complex, use subflows or split it.A normal flow of 25 steps.A normal flow of 5 steps that calls subflows.
7Write the repeat rule after the steps, in plain English.Loop i = 1 to n: add item.Repeat steps 2 to 4 until the Patron has all items.

The SVDPI form is useful for another reason: the subject and the objects of the sentences are the candidate classes of the next chapter. The step “The Patient contacts the office regarding an appointment” suggests three classes: Patient, Office and Appointment. The form is not possible for every step, but use it whenever you can.

Hardest guidelineGuideline 3 is the hardest. First write the step from the point of view of the initiator and of the receiver, and then write the bird's-eye version.

10Creating a use-case description

The textbook gives seven steps. They are in order, but the analyst often goes back and forth between them.

  1. Choose a use case. Use the importance level, or the rating of Larman. Another method is dot voting: each team member gets a few dots and puts them on the use cases that matter most.
  2. Create the overview. Name the primary actor, set the type (Detail, Essential), list the stakeholders and their interests, give the importance, a brief description, the trigger and the relationships. Most of this comes from the use-case diagram.
  3. Describe the normal flow. Write the typical successful path in SVDPI sentences. Visualize doing the steps (role play). The most likely path through the activity diagram is the basis. Do not think about all the possible combinations yet.
  4. Check the normal flow. Are the steps about the same size? Is any step too long or too complex? Work on a whiteboard, and write on the form only when the steps are fairly stable.
  5. Identify the alternate and exceptional flows. Label each with the step it replaces (3a, 3b). Write them in SVDPI form as well.
  6. Review the description. Review it with the users. Look for chances to simplify, merge or split use cases, and to add include, extend or generalization. The best test is a role play: the user follows the written steps like a recipe.
  7. Repeat until done. Users change their minds. Iterate until enough use cases are documented to find the candidate classes. Do not get trapped in the details.
TipStart from the activity diagram. Every activity becomes at least one step, the most likely path becomes the normal flow, and the decisions become subflows and alternate flows.

12Verifying & validating the models

Before we go on to structural and behavioral modeling, we must be sure that the functional models describe the business process faithfully.

Verification
Did we build the models correctly? Are they consistent with each other?
Validation
Did we build the right models? Do they match what the client needs?

Two tools help: walkthroughs and a consistency check.

12.1 Walkthroughs

A walkthrough is a peer review of the models. The team comes from the development team and the client. The purpose is to test the fidelity of the models against the requirements and against each other. A walkthrough finds errors; it does not fix them. The team fixes them after the meeting. Walkthroughs are interactive: the presenter walks through the diagram and the others ask questions, for example “Why is this activity not here?”. Even the act of presenting often helps the author see the error, so read your own models out loud, even if it feels strange.

Table 12.1 · Roles in a walkthrough
RoleTask
PresenterThe person mainly responsible for the model. Presents it to the team.
Recorder (scribe)Takes the minutes. Writes down every error that is found, so that the analysis team can fix it.
Maintenance oracleRaises questions about how easy the model will be to maintain and reuse.
OrganizerCalls the meeting, sets it up and runs it.

For a walkthrough to work, the team must be prepared. Send the materials out in advance and ask everyone to mark them up before the meeting. Otherwise the meeting is slow and not useful.

12.2 Do the three models agree?

The use-case diagram, the activity diagrams and the use-case descriptions describe the same requirements, so they must agree (Figure 12.1). Table 12.2 is a checklist you can use in every walkthrough.

Table 12.2 · Consistency checks between the three models
BetweenCheck
Activity diagram and descriptionEvery activity has at least one event in the description, and every event has an activity.
Every object node is mentioned in an event.
The order of the events is the same as the order of the activities.
Description and use-case diagramThere is one description for each use case, and one use case for each description.
Every actor of the description is on the diagram and is connected to the use case by an association. (Stakeholders can also be actors, depending on the organization.)
Every include, extend and generalization appears in both.
