01Introduction

Companies collect a lot of personal data about individuals. This raises questions that have no simple answer. How should a company respond when someone asks for personal information about its users, and does it have a policy for that? What happens to the personal data of a bankrupt company: is it an asset that is sold on with the rest of the company, and does the privacy agreement end when the business ends?

The chapter names three ways in which data can hurt people:

Errors in the dataA mistake in collecting or processing data can harm an individual, so both must be accurate. People often do not know what was collected about them, whether it is accurate, or whether they can correct it.
Errors in the algorithmEven with good data, the conclusions can be invalid because the algorithm is incorrect or biased. How do we keep algorithm-driven systems from harming people?
Misleading presentationThe results are correct but shown in an over-simple or exaggerated way, for example with a manipulated axis scale, or as one aggregate number that hides a minority group.

Data scientists have a responsibility to make sure that the algorithm is fit for purpose, that it uses the right training data, and, as far as practical, to detect and eliminate unintentional discrimination against individuals or groups. A common cause of unfairness is an algorithm whose criteria are tuned to fit the majority: it can be unfair to minorities.

03Data science & personal data

3.1 What data science is

Information is power in the digital age, so the collection, processing and use of information needs to be regulated. Data science is the extraction of knowledge from data sets that consist of structured and unstructured data. Data scientists have a responsibility to make sure that this knowledge is used wisely and not abused. Data science can be seen as a branch of statistics, because it uses many of its concepts. To prevent errors in the analysis, both the data and the models must be valid.

3.2 Profiles, surveillance and location

Personal data is collected from the way people use computers: their email, their Google searches, their Internet and social media use. From this, companies build revealing profiles of users that can be targeted to advertisers.

Data monetisationAlso called surveillance capitalism. The commercial exploitation of data gathered from the surveillance of users (search history, browsing history…). It is the widespread collection and commodification of personal data by corporations.
Mass surveillanceModern technology lets governments watch their own citizens. Face recognition software can recognise citizens at demonstrations and other mass assemblies.
Location trackingSmartphones provide location data, so the user can be tracked. Such technology must be regulated and not abused by the state.

Privacy has become more important in the information age. It is the way in which we separate ourselves from other people, and it is the right to be left alone. The European GDPR has become an important protector of privacy and personal data, and European and other countries have adopted it.

Example · hiking bootsThe probability that a person buys hiking boots is very low, say 1 in 5000. The person then looks at boots on a site such as Amazon. Now the probability is seen as much higher, say 1 in 100. This jump makes the person interesting to advertisers, and targeted (pop-up) advertisements for hiking boots start to appear. This is tedious for someone who was only browsing. But many apps are free, and their revenue comes from advertisements, so they gather data about the user and sell it on to advertisers.

04Ethics of data science

The use of data has social consequences. The ethics of data science investigates what is fair and ethical in data science, and what should or should not be done with data. Ethical behaviour means using our internal moral compass to form judgments and make decisions.

4.1 Who owns a photo?

If I take a picture of another person, does it belong to me (I own the camera and I collected the data) or to the person in it? Most reasonable people would say that the image is my property. But it contains the personal data (the image) of another person, so I have an ethical responsibility to make sure that it is not misused to harm them. If I do misuse it, I may be open to a lawsuit.

4.2 Ethical rules and legal rules

Ethical rules
Shared values that are followed voluntarily to make the world a better place.
Legal rules
Rules that are used to enforce social values.

Often the benefits of following rules outweigh the costs. The rules of the road give safe and predictable travel. The cost is that every driver must conform: drive under the speed limit and on the correct side of the road.

4.3 Informed consent

A fundamental principle of ethics in data science is informed consent. It comes from the ethics of medical experiments:

  • the individual is informed about the experiment and gives consent voluntarily;
  • the individual can withdraw consent at any time during the experiment;
  • the experiment is generally done to benefit society;
  • a board often approves and oversees the study, checks that all participants gave informed consent, and balances the benefit to society against possible harm to individuals;
  • only after consent may data be collected.

In information technology, the principle appears when a user accepts the terms and conditions of a website. These terms state that data will be collected, processed and shared. But this is not informed consent in the medical sense, for two reasons:

Not informedThe details are hidden in the small print of a long and largely unreadable document.
Not voluntaryA user who wants the software has no choice but to click accept. Otherwise there is no access. Consent is essentially coerced, not freely given.

4.5 Questions on data collection

Data collection is not new: cameras and telephones have existed for many years. People have reasonable expectations of privacy. They do not expect their phone calls to be monitored, or to be recorded in a changing room or at home. The question is whether reasonable rules can be agreed that balance the conflicting rights of the business and the individual.

05Problems in data science

Data science is a multi-disciplinary field. Large data sets (big data, which combines data from many sources such as bar codes, CCTV, shopping data and driving licences) are analysed to extract useful information. The field has great power to harm and to help, and data scientists must use this power wisely.

5.1 Errors in the data

  • The sample may not be representative of the entire population.
  • Incomplete training data: in machine learning, the algorithm may have learned from incomplete data. Training data is also incomplete if the future population differs from the past population.
  • Attributes: the attributes collected are often limited to what is available, and the data scientist must decide what to do with missing attributes.
  • Processing errors, for example when analysing text or recognising faces in photos.
  • Human errors, such as spelling errors or a misunderstood data field.

The trouble with such errors is that decisions are made on the basis of the data, and individuals do not know whether there is a way to correct it.

5.2 Errors in the model

Even with perfect data, the conclusions may be invalid because the model is wrong. Many machine-learning algorithms only estimate parameters to fit a pre-determined model, without knowing whether the model is appropriate. The classic case is fitting a linear model to a non-linear reality. This is most dangerous when extrapolating: estimating values outside the given data.

Simpson’s paradoxA trend that appears in several groups of data disappears or reverses when the groups are combined. So take care before you assign a result from group data to an individual. Think about which population you study, whether to segment it into groups, and how.

5.3 Bias

It seems reasonable to assume that data-driven analysis is fair and neutral. It is not, because humans set the boundary conditions and may introduce bias without meaning to: through the choice of model, through training data that does not represent the population, or because the past population does not represent the future one. The result can be unfair algorithmic decisions; the chapter mentions the infamous Amazon hiring algorithm (discussed in Chapter 7 of the book).

5.4 Correct but misleading

Manipulated scaleThe scales of the axis are chosen so that the result looks exaggerated.
Over-simple resultThe population has two or more groups with different behaviour and one is dominant. The correct aggregate is shown, the differences are hidden, diversity is suppressed, and the minority group is discriminated against.

5.5 Exploration

Exploration is the first phase of data analysis. A hypothesis is devised to fit the observed data. This is the opposite of traditional statistics, where the hypothesis comes first and the data (from control and target groups) is used to confirm or reject it. The exploratory approach must be used carefully to keep the results valid.

06Problems with data science algorithms

They codify the presentAlgorithms tend to learn and codify the current state of the world. It is then harder to change the algorithm to reflect a changing world.
Narrow focusData scientists focus on getting the algorithm to do the right processing, and often do not consider the wider societal impact. New technology affects social groups in different ways, and groups also view privacy differently.
StereotypesA person is put in a group because of one attribute and is then given the opinions or properties of the group: the person is typecast. It is hard to break out, because the bias is embedded in the algorithm and helps to maintain the status quo.
PredictionsPredictions are probabilistic and may be wrong. They only suggest a greater likelihood. Used in predictive policing and the prediction of uprisings, with the danger of false positives and false negatives.

Companies must fully consider the societal consequences of their algorithms, so that the benefits of data science are achieved and harm to individuals is avoided.

07Data analytics

Data analytics is the science of handling data collection by computer-driven systems, where the goal is to generate insights that improve decision-making. It is an overlap of statistics, information technology and domain knowledge, and it is widely used in social media, e-commerce, the Internet of Things, recommendation engines and gaming. It analyses data to create order and patterns, and so turns data into information and insight.

DescriptiveWhat happened?

7.2 What companies use it for

  • Automated pricing (prescriptive): prices set from demand, weather and commodity prices, raised or lowered much faster than a human could. Examples: airline flights and hotel rooms.
  • New product design: drilling down into customer data to learn which features customers want and what price they will pay.
  • Customer loyalty and retention: gathering customers’ opinions from social media, email and phone calls, so that the voice of the customer is heard and acted on.
  • Marketing: measuring how successful a campaign was, and running targeted campaigns to segmented audiences.
  • Targeted suggestions: Netflix collects searches and viewing history and uses them to suggest the next movie.
Market segmentationDividing the audience into subsets by their characteristics: demographics, interests, needs and location. It helps to know the customers and what each segment needs.
Big data analyticsExamining large amounts of data to find hidden patterns and correlations and so make the right business decisions.

08Business intelligence, big data & data mining

8.1 Business analytics and business intelligence

Management decisions depend on the accuracy and completeness of the information managers have; inaccurate or incomplete information leads to poor decisions. Company data is often unstructured or in diverse formats, which is harder to gather and analyse. That is why software firms offer business intelligence solutions that unify a company’s data and analytics.

Business analyticsConverting business data into useful business information with statistical techniques and advanced software. A set of analytical methods for solving problems and helping decisions. It combines analysis with intuition.
Business intelligence (BI)Processes all the data generated by a business and turns it into clear reports (e.g. a dashboard of key metrics), trends and performance measures for management. It is “data analytics with insight”. It may use data mining, performance benchmarking, process analysis and descriptive analytics.

8.2 Big data

Big data means large, diverse sets of data that arrive at ever-increasing rates and volumes. It is described by the three V’s:

Volumehow much data there is
Velocitythe speed at which it is created and collected
Varietythe scope of the data points covered

The volume is now counted in zettabytes (ZB). A zettabyte is 1 sextillion bytes = 270 bytes: about a billion terabytes, 1000 exabytes, or a trillion gigabytes.

8.3 Data mining and data warehouses

Big data often comes from data mining: exploring and analysing large blocks of data to find meaningful patterns and trends. Organisations load the data into a data warehouse, which means the data is centralised in a single database or program, stored on in-house servers or in the cloud. The user decides how to organise the data, application software sorts it, and it is presented in an easy format such as a graph or report.

Structured data
Often already managed in the organisation’s databases or spreadsheets. May be numeric and is easily formatted.
Unstructured data
Unformatted, free form: it does not fit a predetermined format. It may come from search engines or forum discussions on social media.

Data may also be internal or external. Big data is collected from publicly shared comments on social media, from apps, questionnaires, product purchases and so on. It is generally stored in databases and analysed with software built for large and complex data sets.

09Social media analytics & the mathematics

9.1 Analytics for social media

Social media companies use data analytics to get a quantitative insight into human behaviour on a site. The business understands its audience better, improves the user experience and creates content that interests the users. It is learning to read a social media community through data: collecting, monitoring, analysing, summarising and drawing graphs.

MetricsQuantifiable data on activities, events and conversations: number of likes, number of followers, number of times a video is played on YouTube.
Social listeningMonitoring keywords and mentions in social media conversations of the target audience and industry, to understand what the audience says about the business. It gathers opinions and emotions and lets the business engage with its audience.
Social media campaignA marketing tool: a company uses social media platforms to interact with customers. Designing it is iterative: first set the objectives (e.g. more users, build a brand), design the campaign, measure, refine, and repeat the cycle.
KPIsFor a campaign: more followers or subscribers, or an increase in content shared.

Facebook keeps metrics such as the number of page views, the number of likes and the reach of posts. Reach is the number of people who saw a post at least once. Twitter has a dashboard that summarises how successful tweet activity has been, and the interests and locations of the user’s followers.

10Data privacy

10.1 Argus and the Panopticon

In Greek mythology, Argus Panoptes was an all-seeing giant with one hundred eyes. Some eyes stayed open even when he slept, so he was the perfect guardian. Hermes, the messenger of the gods, disguised himself as a shepherd, put all of Argus’s eyes to sleep, and killed him.

Jeremy Bentham, the founder of utilitarianism, designed a circular prison in the eighteenth century called the Panopticon. A single guard in the centre could observe all of the prisoners. A prisoner could not know whether he was being watched at a given moment, so he would behave as if he were watched all of the time.

The modern Panopticon is a set of security cameras, websites that monitor user behaviour, or the entire Internet working out everything we do.

Should we care, if we do nothing wrong? Some argue that with zero privacy everyone would be completely honest, because everyone could know what everyone else does. Others answer that privacy is a basic human right and is needed for freedom to be exercised in society.

10.4 De-identification

De-identification is the removal of identifiable information from data: fields such as name, address and phone number, so that no personally identifiable attribute remains. The person is then not immediately identifiable, which gives some safeguard. But the identity may still be worked out from the fields that remain, so care is needed before public records are released. The chapter concludes that anonymity is limited or virtually impossible, given how much public and private information exists, and given how fast facial recognition can now identify people from images.

11What is privacy?

11.1 Three definitions

  • the right to be left alone;
  • secret or intimate information being kept secure from others;
  • control over one’s personal information: the individual decides what is shared, when it is shared and how it is shared.

Users should be in control of how their data is used. But most user agreements are “all-or-nothing”: the user must give up control of the data to use the application at all. The user is coerced. Users would be happier with graduated choices, so that they could make trade-offs and choose a level of privacy they are comfortable with.

11.2 Privacy is an old concern

Privacy concerns grew out of early technologies: the first cameras, microphones and telephones made indiscreet or unauthorised images and recordings possible.

The earliest concern was the security and confidentiality of a message, which led people to use ciphers. Julius Caesar used an alphabetic cipher during his campaign in Gaul in the first century B.C., and the emperor Augustus did something similar. Some leaders of the American War of Independence used codes and pseudonyms.

Democracies offer a peaceful way to replace an unpopular government and usually have laws that protect citizens against unreasonable police searches. Totalitarian states are often ruthless: a culture of surveillance, identifying people who are a threat to the regime and removing them by imprisonment or assassination, police searches, seizure of property and police brutality.

11.3 Databases and the fair information principles

Privacy became important in IT in the early 1970s, with the introduction of databases that could hold private information about people. The US Secretary’s Advisory Committee on Automated Personal Data Systems published the report Records, Computers and the Rights of Citizens in 1973, with a set of fair information processing principles (FIPs). According to the chapter this led to the Fair Information Practice Principles Act in 1974, the basis on which data collection is governed in the United States.

Table 11.1 · Principles of data collection (Table 5.5 of the chapter)

11.4 Privacy today

Technology has moved from mainframes and databases to smartphones, social media, the Internet of Things and AI. Users share large parts of their lives with a massive online audience and with corporations. The chapter asks: where does the data go, who controls it, are breaches managed, is the data encrypted, is there transparency?

It is often unclear who collects personal information, what is collected, what is done with it and with whom it is shared. Theft of personal information gives the thief power over the individual: the stolen information can be used against the person or to commit a crime. Vehicles that connect to the Internet can be hacked, and a hacker could take over steering and brakes.

12Social media

Social media is the use of computer technology for the creation and exchange of user-generated content. It is designed to make the individual share personal information; every post reveals a little more. It is also designed to be addictive, and it poses risks to privacy.

A user’s information has two distinct audiences: other users and the platform itself. Both can harm the user’s privacy. There is a power imbalance between the platform, with its immense databases, and the user.

Users often do not realise the full extent of their audience: friends, family, employer, colleagues and nameless others. People who are authorised in your network may still not be the desired recipients of a post. Online messages are hard to delete, and a destructive post can last long after the incident. So it is in the user’s interest to keep posts discreet.

13Internet of Things

The Internet of Things (IoT) is not a single technology. It is a collection of devices, sensors and services that capture data to monitor and control the world around them: cars, clothing, fridges, fitness monitors. It lets everyday devices connect to other devices or to people over the Internet.

Many connected devices with microphones and cameras mean more eyes watching the individual and more points of failure. IoT therefore poses greater risks than basic computers. The two risks are security risks and privacy risks.

Hardware failure
Hardware is generally more reliable than software. It tends to fail because components wear out over time.
Software failure
Usually the result of design issues. Software needs regular updates to correct problems and close security vulnerabilities.

The Internet connection cuts both ways: it makes software upgrades possible, but it also means the device can be targeted by hackers, just like a computer.

  • Security: many IoT devices are inexpensive and have serious security vulnerabilities. Some do not encrypt data or images sent to the cloud, so an eavesdropper can intercept the traffic.
  • Privacy: the devices produce granular personal data, such as when the person is at home and what the person eats, and this data may be shared with other devices or platforms.
  • Data should be gathered only with user consent.

14AI & facial recognition

Facial recognition is an AI technology with two faces. It can protect the individual: it unlocks phones and authenticates identity. But it can now recognise individuals at demonstrations and street protests, so authoritarian governments may use it as a tool of control. Surveillance combined with facial recognition can be oppressive and lead to a totalitarian state. A society of constant surveillance, where technology monitors daily activity, recognises patterns and draws inferences, is moving towards totalitarianism. (Orwell’s 1949 novel Nineteen Eighty-Four describes such a state.) It may challenge civil liberties and could severely affect marginalised groups.

Table 14.1 · Why facial recognition has become popular
#Reason
1Faces are central to identity
2Faces are hard to hide
3There are existing face and name databases
4Facial recognition is widespread

How it works: it is a biometric technology. It analyses visual data from social media and other sources, detects facial features and essentially reduces each face to a mathematical equation, using factors such as the distance between the eyes and the width of the nose. The patterns are compared with patterns in facial recognition databases to confirm identity.

Some companies use it to read facial expressions during job interviews, which gives them data they would not otherwise receive.

Deep fakesAn AI technology that creates convincing images and videos of people doing or saying things they never did or said. It uses content taken from social media and other media, manipulated and edited. It could show a person committing a crime. So far it has been used mainly for humour, as in political satire, but as it improves it may become difficult to tell real from fake, with serious consequences for society.

15Privacy and the law

Data collection laws focus on how data is collected, used and shared. Data protection includes the right to information self-determination: the freedom of individuals to decide what happens to their personal data and the purpose of its collection. A privacy policy says what personal data will be collected, how it is processed, used and shared, and what can be done about it. An individual can sue another for a tort, for example for prying or stalking, a defamatory article, or a violation of privacy.

The mediaProtects against intrusion (phone tapping, snooping, examining a bank account). The tort of public disclosure of private facts stops others from spreading private facts when the matter is not of legitimate concern to the public. Slander and libel laws protect a person’s good name against defamation.
SurveillanceSearch warrants are needed in most countries to search a private home and seize property. Warrants are generally needed for records held by telecommunication companies (calls, and metadata such as date, time, duration and geo-location) and by Internet companies (emails, sites visited, searches, messages).
Personal dataRules on how companies collect, use, share and protect personal data.

Countries differ, but in general:

  • the security and privacy commitments in a company’s policies must be fully implemented;
  • companies should be held accountable for security breaches that compromise privacy, and may be liable for losses suffered by individuals;
  • people must not be misled about the functionality of a website or app that puts their security or privacy at risk;
  • users must consent to changes in the privacy policy that would allow more personal data to be collected;
  • users must be informed about the extensiveness of tracking and data collection.

16EU GDPR privacy law

The European General Data Protection Regulation (EU GDPR 2016/679) is a comprehensive data protection framework that became operational in 2018. In the EU, privacy and data protection are regarded as fundamental human rights. The goal of GDPR is to give individuals control over their personal data.

It has had a huge impact around the world: other countries used it to develop similar laws, for example Japan and the state of California.

GDPR prohibits the transfer of personal data outside the EU to countries that do not provide an equivalent or adequate data protection framework. Its protections follow the data.

16.1 Processing is presumed illegitimate

GDPR applies whenever personal data is processed, and it starts from the presumption that the processing is illegitimate. So companies carry the burden: they must show a legitimate basis. The chapter names three:

  1. the company has the consent of the data subject; or
  2. the processing is necessary because of a contract between the company and the data subject; or
  3. the company has a legitimate interest, and the interest of the data controller prevails over that of the data subject.

16.2 Four fair information practices

Data qualityData must be obtained legitimately and used for the purpose for which it was acquired.
TransparencyOpenness, so that individuals know how their data will be used.
Sensitive dataSpecial protection, with the ability to opt in for consent (e.g. race, sexual orientation).
EnforcementStandards of enforcement to ensure compliance.

The Data Privacy Impact Assessment (DPIA) is part of GDPR. It is needed when processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. The standard for informed consent is very high: it must be freely given and informed.

16.3 Rights of the data subject

Accesssee the data
Portabilitytake the data elsewhere
Rectifycorrect the data
Erasehave the data deleted
Objectobject to processing
Restrictrestrict processing

Course-text wording: see the clarifications on dates and legal scope.

According to the chapter, the EU Data Protection Commissioner (DPC) is responsible for monitoring the application of GDPR and upholding the rights of EU citizens. Several large technology companies, for example Amazon, Facebook and Google, have received large fines in the EU for violations.

17EU Digital Services Act

Course-text wording: see the clarifications on dates and legal scope.

The European Digital Services Act (DSA), in the chapter’s words, became part of European law in 2024 and applies to all member states of the European Union. Its goal is to protect the digital space against illegal content and to protect the fundamental rights of users. It applies to social media companies and the online marketplace. In short: a set of rules for a safer online space. Platforms are held accountable for their role in spreading harmful content.

18Security of data

Privacy can be violated if system security is weak, so good cybersecurity is a prerequisite for the protection of personal data. The security of a system is its ability to protect itself from attack. A secure system preserves three properties of the data:

ConfidentialityInformation may be viewed and accessed only by those authorised. Tools: encryption (data is meaningless to anyone else) and access controls.
IntegrityData may be modified only by those authorised to do so.
AvailabilityThe system and its data are available for use at all times; it is not subject to a denial-of-service attack.

Attacks can be internal or external to the company. They may lead to unauthorised data access and usage, theft and deletion, or manipulation.

The system must be designed for security; loopholes can be introduced during development. Risk analysis gives a risk profile: threats and vulnerabilities with their probability and impact. The high-risk areas lead to the security requirements. There is a trade-off between security risks and the cost of security measures, and it is a continuous process because technology keeps changing.

PreventiveStop unauthorised attacks before they succeed and do harm.Encryption of communication and stored data. 19Summary

  • The ethics of data science asks what is fair and ethical, and what should or should not be done with data.
  • Its fundamental principle is informed consent: the individual is informed about the purpose of data collection and consents voluntarily. Online, consent is usually given by accepting terms and conditions.
  • Companies build revealing profiles of users from their use of computers, and these may be sold on to advertisers.
  • Governments can conduct mass surveillance; face recognition can recognise citizens at demonstrations.
  • Privacy is the way we separate ourselves from other people, and it is the right to be left alone. Location tracking and other technology challenge it.
  • The GDPR has become an important protector of privacy and personal data, in Europe and beyond.
