01What software engineering is

Software engineering is the discipline concerned with developing software. It covers requirements gathering and definition, design and development, and testing to verify that the software is correct. It is a team activity with roles such as project managers, system analysts, developers and testers. The book also calls it the multi-person construction of multi-version programs.

IEEE 610.12 definitionSoftware engineering is the application of a systematic, disciplined, quantifiable approach to the development, operation and maintenance of software; that is, the application of engineering to software, and the study of such approaches.

According to the chapter, software engineering includes:

  1. Methodologies to design, develop and test software that meets customers’ needs.
  2. Software that is engineered: properly designed, developed and tested by sound engineering principles.
  3. Quality and safety that are properly addressed.
  4. Mathematics to help design and verify the software. How much depends on how safety critical the product is: peer reviews and rigorous testing are often enough, and heavy mathematics is kept for safety- and security-critical software.
  5. Sound project management and quality management practices.
  6. Proper support and maintenance of the software.

Software engineering is not just programming. The engineer states precisely what the product must do, so it is clear what is and is not required. The requirements are rigorously reviewed and the design is validated. Then code is written, and peer reviews and testing verify and validate it. For safety-critical systems this verification is extra rigorous and may use mathematical techniques. Dijkstra and Hoare argued that correct software should be derived from its specification with mathematics, with a mathematical proof of correctness.

The title “engineer”

In classical engineering the title is earned on merit through education and competence, and it brings a duty to behave professionally and ethically. In computing, “software engineer” is often used loosely for anyone who builds things.

David ParnasArgued that computer scientists should be educated as engineers and use mathematics. Software engineers are responsible for safe, high-quality software, are accountable for their decisions, must object to decisions that violate professional standards, and should be licensed like other engineers. He also introduced information hiding in the 1970s.
Professional bodiesMembership of a professional engineering body requires following its code of ethics. Professional software engineers follow best practice and the defined software processes.
TrainingCompanies invest in training for each role (project management, design, testing, service management). A company that ignores its staff’s competence gets poor results and loses reputation and market share.

03Lifecycles & quality practices

The lifecycle used on a project is chosen to fit the needs of that project. The chapter briefly reviews the best-known ones:

WaterfallFor projects where the requirements are known early. Phases: requirements, specification, design, coding, unit, integration, system and acceptance tests. Each phase has entry and exit criteria. (W. Royce, 1970)
SpiralDeveloped by Barry Boehm in the 1980s for requirements that are not fully known or that evolve. Each spiral adds functionality: objectives and risk analysis, updated requirements, design, code, test and a user review.
Rational Unified ProcessDeveloped at Rational (now IBM) in the late 1990s. Uses UML, the visual modelling language created by Rumbaugh, Booch and Jacobson for specifying, constructing and documenting object-oriented systems.
AgileA lightweight methodology, more responsive to customer needs, with a strong collaborative style.
Halfway through the projectWaterfall: 100% of the requirements are about 50% done. Agile: 50% of the requirements are about 100% done.
  • CMMI: a framework, used by thousands of organisations, for implementing best practice in software and systems engineering and defining high-quality processes.
  • Software inspections (the Fagan methodology, Gilb’s approach) build quality into software and reduce the cost of poor quality.
  • Companies that follow CMMI or ISO 9001 use audits to check that processes were followed. Auditors report to management and the findings are addressed.

04The ethical software engineer

Technical decisions are made all the time, and they often affect people’s lives, sometimes harmfully. So the ethical impact of a decision has to be part of the engineering process: the ethical engineer examines both the technical and the ethical dimensions of decisions that affect society.

Do no harmThe fundamental principle, taken from the Hippocratic Oath.
Do not take bribesDecisions must not be bought.
Be fair to othersTreat clients, colleagues and the public fairly.

The Volkswagen scandal (section 12) breached “do no harm”: it deceived the public and harmed society, the company, its shareholders and its employees. It was both unethical and illegal.

The codes of ethics of the IEEE, ACM and BCS (chapter 3) guide how technology and values interact. Engineers must know their ethical responsibilities throughout development and act whenever ethical standards are in danger of being violated.

Liability and qualifications (chapter footnotes)

  • An individual programmer is unlikely to be sued over a flaw. Software is a team effort, so it is hard to prove one engineer was negligent, and most software comes with a disclaimer rather than a guarantee of quality.
  • Companies are more likely to be sued: they are legally responsible for their employees’ actions and are wealthier. For bespoke software, more protection for the customer can be written into the contract.
  • The BCS introduced qualifications for IT professionals. The most important is the ISEB (Information Systems Examination Board), covering service management, project management, software testing and more.

05Safety and ethics

Releasing unreliable software can damage property or injure (even kill) third parties, so companies must be confident that a product is fit for purpose before release. Widely used software must be dependable: available when needed, and operating safely and reliably without adverse side effects. With billions of connected devices, it must also be secure and able to deal with and recover from attacks.

DefinitionA safety critical system is a system whose failure could result in significant economic damage or loss of life. Examples: aircraft flight control systems, nuclear power stations, missile systems.
  • Rigorous processes are essential. Testing alone is often insufficient to verify these systems.
  • The safety-critical industry treats any change to the software as creating a new program, which must again be shown to be reliable and safe, so extensive testing follows.
  • Formal verification and model checking can give extra assurance of correctness.

06Therac-25 disaster

The Therac-25 was a computer-controlled radiation therapy machine (a linear accelerator) built by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL) in the early 1980s. It treated cancer by aiming a particle beam at malignant tissue.

On the earlier Therac-20, safety checks were done in hardware. On the Therac-25, software took over many of these safety-critical checks. The software was responsible for monitoring the machine’s status, accepting treatment input, setting the machine up, turning the beam on and off, and detecting hardware malfunctions.

Between 1985 and 1987 there were six major accidents: patients received doses hundreds of times too high. Three people died and three were seriously injured. The machine stayed in use for over 18 months after the first accident because AECL believed an accident was impossible.

AccidentAECL’s response
1stNo action; an accident was believed to be impossible.
2nd (a month later)Sent an engineer who could not reproduce the problem. Made some hardware and software changes and claimed the problem was solved and reliability increased many times.
3rdDenied that the Therac-25 could have caused it.
4thBlamed a wiring problem.
5thTogether with FDA investigations, AECL finally launched a thorough investigation.

The FDA ruled the machines defective and told AECL to prepare a corrective action plan and inform its customers. The plan worried the FDA: there was a lack of software engineering documentation and the testing was inadequate. The FDA directed AECL to test the system extensively every time even a small software change was made.

Main reasons for the disaster
  • Initial failure to believe end users
  • Overconfidence of engineers in its correctness
  • Poor software design and development practices
  • Inadequate testing
  • Poor resolution of software defects
  • Software errors

Software engineering was immature in the 1980s, but the book says this is no excuse. Common sense required a proper investigation after the first accident, and all machines should have been treated as unsafe until proved otherwise and withdrawn until the cause was found and fixed.

07Space Shuttle Challenger

In January 1986 the Challenger broke apart 73 seconds into its flight and all seven crew members were killed. The Rogers Commission found the cause: the O-ring seals on a joint of the right solid rocket booster (SRB) failed, because record low temperatures had stiffened the rubber and reduced its ability to seal. The commission also called the launch decision process deeply flawed, with conflicts between engineering data and management judgment.

The night before the launch

  1. Boisjoly, an engineer at Morton Thiokol (the SRB contractor; the book spells it “Thiokel” and calls him Robert, his real first name was Roger), strongly objected: he knew the O-rings erode and fail at low temperature.
  2. On the evening conference call the whole Thiokol team recommended a postponement. Their minimum launch temperature was 52 °F, and the forecast was 30 °F overnight.
  3. They expected NASA to rubber-stamp the decision. Instead NASA said the briefing was based on emotion rather than factual data and asked Thiokol to review the data again.
  4. Every subcontractor had to sign off. NASA seems to have pushed Thiokol to recommend launching unless they could prove it was unsafe.
  5. Thiokol management met without Boisjoly, decided to proceed, and told NASA its data was inconclusive. Boisjoly later called this decision unethical.
SeparatismEngineers and scientists give technical input, and management decides, weighing all inputs into a value judgment. It usually works, but fails when managers balance conflicting values (a strict deadline vs. safety) and believe, or push others to believe, that the risk is “small but manageable”.
Precautionary principleA course of action must be shown to be safe before it is carried out. This was NASA’s normal practice, but the night before the launch NASA reversed the burden of proof: Thiokol had to prove it was unsafe.

Decisions need openness and transparency, must rest on objective facts and data, and must keep risks minimal and manageable. Once his input was ignored, Boisjoly arguably had a moral responsibility to blow the whistle. He may instead have reasoned, following separatism, that he had warned management and the decision was theirs.

The costAll seven crew died, among them the schoolteacher Christa McAuliffe, chosen through NASA’s Teacher in Space project to teach lessons from space and inspire students’ interest in science.

08Ethical project management

A project is a temporary group activity with a specific goal, such as delivering a product, and a clearly defined beginning and end. Project management covers planning and estimation, resources, issues and change requests, quality, risks, budget, progress monitoring and corrective action, communication with stakeholders, and delivering a high-quality product on time and on budget.

The Project Management Institute (PMI) has a code of ethics and professional conduct. Its four core values:

ResponsibilityOwn your decisions and actions, including those you fail to make or take. Accept only work you are competent for, keep commitments, fix errors promptly, protect proprietary information, report unethical or illegal conduct, know the relevant laws.
RespectBe sensitive to people from other cultures, behave professionally, listen and try to understand others, and work through conflicts.
FairnessDecide objectively and impartially. Stay out of decisions where you have a conflict of interest. No favouritism or discrimination.
HonestyBe truthful in communication and conduct. Never make or condone misleading or false statements.
  • Larger projects offer more chances for ethics to be compromised than smaller ones.
  • Balancing budget, schedule, effort and quality can tempt a manager to cut corners, compromising quality, health and safety, or privacy.
  • Example: choosing a subcontractor the manager knows from earlier work or from family is a conflict of interest, so the manager should step out of the supplier selection.
  • Good project governance enables ethical project management by keeping key stakeholders regularly informed of status and key decisions.

09Bridge on the River Kwai

Ethical design asks how a technology will be used and whether it could harm people and society. Because the design decides how a technology is used, the design process needs an ethical dimension alongside the desired functionality.

David Lean directed The Bridge on the River Kwai in 1957. The film is based on the building of the Thailand–Burma railway during the Japanese occupation of Burma in the Second World War. (It was actually filmed on the Kelani river near Kitulgala, in Ceylon, today Sri Lanka.)

  • British prisoners of war are ordered to build a bridge. At first they and their leader, Colonel Nicholson, resist.
  • Nicholson then becomes obsessed with building a proper bridge that will outlast the war and show the skill of British engineers.
  • On opening day, as the first train is due, he finally sees what he has done, collaborating with the enemy and helping its further aggression, and blows up the bridge.
LessonThe purpose of the technology must be considered, not only how well it is built. A finished bridge would have helped Japanese aggression spread to other countries, and collaborating with the enemy conflicted with Nicholson’s duty to his country and the army.

10Ethical design & value centred design

Software design translates required functions into a blueprint of a system that can fulfil them. There are usually several design options, and they differ in how well they support particular ethical values. The goal is to choose the design that best meets the most important ethical values and technical considerations. Design activities: problem analysis, requirements analysis and definition (maybe prototyping), architectural design (options and decisions), low-level design, implementation, testing and maintenance.

DefinitionValue centred design (VCD) takes human values into account during design and solves value conflicts through engineering design and technological innovation. It finds the values relevant to the project, understands their conflicts in order to make trade-offs, analyses how well each design meets them, and develops innovative designs for the most relevant values.

Value centred design involves:

  1. Reasoning about and clarifying the values behind conflicting design requirements
  2. Social cost–benefit analysis (including monetary costs for safety)
  3. Evaluation criteria (value criteria; weightings may be used)
  4. Thresholds for what is acceptable on each criterion
  5. Evaluation of the options
  6. The selected option

When different designs score well on different criteria, designers cannot do justice to all values at once. Such moral dilemmas are often resolved by balancing the competing values.

Trade-off decisionA choice between at least two options, where at least two moral values are relevant as choice criteria.

Designers are responsible for ethical designs that satisfy the requirements, are robust and protect public safety. Each option should be rated on the relevant ethical values as well as on technical criteria. Values the chapter lists:

safety accessibility usability sustainability privacy security honesty fairness loyalty

11Data, bias, addiction & security

Data managementProtect personal data ownership, data rights, access rights, privacy and security rights. Follow the legal requirements and best practice when collecting, processing and protecting data.
AccessibilityDesign for different users: people with visual or hearing impairments, or with different levels of language ability or education.
Honesty and legalityAn ethical design gives an open and accurate account of the system and meets all relevant legal and regulatory requirements. Volkswagen’s defeat device is the counter-example.
Unbiased algorithmsAlgorithms must not discriminate against minorities, especially AI pattern-matching ones. Examples: Amazon’s hiring algorithm discriminated against women; a predictive policing algorithm led to racial profiling. (More in chapter 6.)
Addictive designSocial media and other apps are deliberately designed to be addictive, capturing attention at a primal level for the company’s financial gain. People check their phones hundreds of times a day. Should consequences like this count in ethical decisions?
Environmental responsibilitySome cloud companies used AI and machine learning to build visual maps showing Big Oil where to drill for more fossil fuels. Companies must not turn a blind eye to how their products are used.

Security by design

  • Design for security from the start: it is difficult to add security after implementation. Security engineering builds systems that prevent malicious attacks and recover from them.
  • Do a risk assessment of security threats early. It produces security (and privacy) requirements that design and development must implement.
  • Security testing, including penetration testing, looks for flaws and verifies requirements such as confidentiality, availability and integrity.
  • Passing security testing does not guarantee there are no vulnerabilities. Hackers will keep trying.

12Volkswagen emissions scandal

In “Dieselgate”, Volkswagen deliberately programmed its turbocharged direct injection (TDI) diesel engines to switch on their emission controls only during laboratory emission tests. The cars passed the US federal NOx limits in the lab, while according to the chapter, real-world emissions were over 40 times higher.

FactDetail
VehiclesOver 11 million worldwide, about half a million in the US, from 2009 to 2015
DiscoveryIn 2014 differences between European and US models became evident and regulators in several countries investigated
Guilty plea2017: criminal charges; admitted developing a “defeat device” and deliberately concealing it
Fine$2.8 billion for rigging the cars to cheat the tests
Total costAbout $33 billion in fines, penalties, settlements and buybacks by mid-2020
PeopleCEO Martin Winterkorn resigned in 2015 and was charged with fraud and conspiracy in the US in 2018. Herbert Diess became CEO in 2018, helped restore the reputation and led the move to electric vehicles.

The scandal showed that software-controlled machinery is prone to cheating. It opened a debate on whether software used to meet legal or regulatory requirements should be independently verified: published for regulators to inspect and/or independently certified.

It also showed corporate ethics failing to stop the pursuit of business self-interest. An organisation’s code of ethics is sometimes just “window dressing” for the public, not part of daily work.

Why did no engineer speak out?

The book asks: why did the engineers not consider their ethical responsibilities or question the device? Why were there no whistleblowers? Was there a lack of moral courage, no structure for raising ethical concerns, or a total failure of corporate governance? Its possible reasons:

  • Corporate environments focus on the business and the product, not on critical reflection about the technology’s wider effects.
  • Engineers are busy with their lives and building a career, and speaking out is not seen as career-advancing.
  • Hierarchical, command-driven organisations do not encourage speaking out, and subordinates fear the consequences.
  • Engineers focus on making the software meet its specification and may not think about its wider social impact.

Considering the bigger picture is in the interest of both companies and employees. Otherwise they pay later through reputational damage and financial loss.

13Ethical software testing

Testers are professionals and must behave ethically. The ISTQB Code of Ethics for test professionals is based on the IEEE and ACM codes. Certified software testers shall:

  1. act consistently in the public interest;
  2. act in the best interests of their client and employer;
  3. ensure their deliverables meet the highest professional standards;
  4. maintain independence and integrity in professional judgment;
  5. promote an ethical approach to the management of software testing;
  6. advance the integrity and reputation of the profession;
  7. be supportive of colleagues and cooperate with software developers;
  8. participate in lifelong learning and promote ethics in the profession.
  • It is impossible to test everything in the time available, so testing focuses on the areas of greatest risk and the parts users use most. Testers need the right expertise, test environment, test plans and specifications, and tools.
  • Ethical issues arise when the project is behind schedule and the test team is pressed to keep the original delivery date. Too little time can mean serious defects are missed. The test manager must resist pressure that risks quality and raise concerns at senior level.
  • The customer must be told about all quality problems so they can manage the risks.
  • The final test report summarises the testing done, the results, open problems, the problem arrival rate and known risks. It usually ends with the test manager’s release recommendation, based on the facts, with a clear statement that all risks can be managed.
  • If the project manager wants to release on schedule but the test manager thinks it is unsafe, the decision must rest on the facts and risks, supported by objective data.

14Key takeaways & review questions

  • Technical decisions have ethical dimensions; the ethical engineer examines both.
  • “Do no harm” (Hippocratic Oath) is the fundamental principle; also no bribes and fairness.
  • Therac-25: software replaced hardware safety checks; disbelief and overconfidence led to 3 deaths and 3 serious injuries.
  • Challenger: O-rings failed in the cold; management overruled engineering data and reversed the burden of proof.
  • PMI values: responsibility, respect, fairness, honesty.
  • River Kwai: consider the purpose of a technology, not only its quality.
  • Value centred design builds human values into design and resolves conflicts by trade-offs and innovation.
  • Volkswagen: a software “defeat device” cheated emission tests; illegal, unethical and with no whistleblowers.
  • Testers follow the ISTQB code, resist schedule pressure and report all quality problems.

Review questions from the chapter (with short sample answers)

1. What is ethical software engineering?

Software engineering in which the engineer examines both the technical and the ethical dimensions of decisions that affect society, follows the professional codes of ethics (do no harm, no bribes, be fair) and acts when ethical standards are in danger.

2. Explain how the Therac-25 disaster occurred.

Software took over safety checks that the Therac-20 did in hardware. Software errors caused massive radiation overdoses in six accidents (1985–87). AECL disbelieved users, was overconfident, and its poor design practice, inadequate testing and poor defect resolution kept the machine in use for over 18 months.

3. Explain how the Challenger disaster occurred.

Record cold stiffened the O-rings on the right SRB so they failed to seal. Thiokol engineers recommended postponing (minimum 52 °F, forecast 30 °F), but NASA demanded proof it was unsafe, Thiokol management overruled its engineers, and the shuttle broke apart after 73 s, killing all seven crew.

4. What is ethical software design?

Design that considers how the technology will be used and whether it could cause harm, rates every option on ethical values (safety, privacy, accessibility, fairness…) as well as technical criteria, and is honest, legal, unbiased and secure.

5. What is value centred design?

An approach that takes human values into account during design and solves value conflicts through engineering design and innovation: clarify values, cost–benefit analysis, weighted criteria and thresholds, evaluate options, select one.

6. What is ethical software testing?

Testing according to the ISTQB code: acting in the public interest with independence and integrity, focusing on the highest risks, resisting schedule pressure that threatens quality, and reporting all quality problems and risks honestly to the customer.

7. What is ethical project management?

Managing a project according to the PMI values of responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty: not cutting corners on quality or safety, avoiding conflicts of interest, and keeping stakeholders informed through good governance.

8. Explain the concept of separatism.

Engineers provide technical input and managers decide. It fails when managers balance conflicting values such as a deadline against safety and treat a real risk as small and manageable, as before the Challenger launch.

9. What are the ethical considerations in developing safety critical systems?

Failure can cost lives, so rigorous processes are needed beyond testing (formal verification, model checking). Any change counts as a new program that must be shown safe again, and the precautionary principle applies: prove it is safe before going ahead.

10. Explain the ethical considerations in the Volkswagen Dieselgate scandal.

Engineers built a defeat device to deceive regulators and the public, harming the environment and society. No one blew the whistle (hierarchy, career fears, focus on the spec), and the code of ethics was window dressing. It raised the question of independent verification of regulatory software.

11. Explain the ethical considerations in the film “Bridge on the River Kwai”.

Colonel Nicholson built an excellent bridge but ignored its purpose: helping the enemy’s aggression. Engineers must consider what their technology will be used for, and collaborating with the enemy conflicted with his duty to his country.
