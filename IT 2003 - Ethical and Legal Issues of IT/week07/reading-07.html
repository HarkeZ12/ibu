01Introduction

Every app, website and smart device collects data about us: what we search, where we go, who we talk to, even our faces and fingerprints. Lecture 7 asks three questions. What is data privacy and why does it matter? What is ethically right when we collect and use personal data? And what do the laws require?

The lecture has three parts, and this reading follows them:

  1. Data privacy: the idea of privacy, its history, and the threats of the digital age, with case studies (Sections 3–6).
  2. Ethical aspects: the principles, ethical dimensions, technical tools, individual rights and Privacy by Design (Sections 7–11).
  3. Legal aspects: privacy laws, the EU GDPR, the EU Digital Services Act and laws in other countries, plus best practices for IT (Sections 12–15).

03What privacy is

3.1 The right to be left alone

The classic definition comes from Louis Brandeis and Samuel Warren in 1890: privacy is “the right to be left alone”.

In 1960 William Prosser described four types of privacy invasion:

Type of invasionWhat it meansExample
Intrusion upon seclusionIntruding into someone’s private space or affairs.A camera hidden in a changing room.
Public disclosure of private factsMaking true but private facts public.Posting someone’s medical diagnosis online.
False or misleading publicityPresenting a person in a false light.A photo used next to a story it has nothing to do with.
Misappropriation of name or likenessUsing someone’s name or face for your own benefit.A celebrity photo used in an ad without permission.

Privacy problems are old. In a small place everyone knows everyone’s business; in a large place you can be anonymous. The modern issue is different: the Internet is not forgetful. Data lives forever.

3.2 Three ways to think about privacy

ViewMeaning
SecrecyKeeping intimate information secure.
ControlDeciding when and how your data is shared.
Contextual integrityInformation should flow appropriately for its context (Helen Nissenbaum, 2004). Medical data shared with your doctor is fine; the same data sent to your employer is not.
Definitions

Data privacy = the protection of personal information collected by digital systems.

De-identification = removing identifiable information from data, such as the name, address and phone number fields, so that no person can be identified.

3.3 Why privacy is so important

  • It protects freedom, autonomy and identity.
  • It prevents discrimination and manipulation.
  • It enables trust in digital services.

Without privacy there is a risk of chilling effects, social control and surveillance states.

DefinitionThe chilling effect is when people or groups hold back from expressing themselves because they fear running afoul of a law or regulation. Example: people stop searching for or posting about a topic because they think they are being watched.

04Roots of data privacy: the Panopticon and the 1973 report

4.1 The Panopticon metaphor

In Greek mythology Argus Panoptes was an all-seeing giant with one hundred eyes looking in every direction. In the eighteenth century the philosopher Jeremy Bentham designed a circular prison, the Panopticon, where a single guard in the centre could watch all the prisoners. The prisoners never knew whether they were being watched, so they behaved as if they always were.

Nowadays the Panopticon is a set of security cameras watching people, websites monitoring user behaviour, or even the entire Internet.

4.2 The 1973 report and the Fair Information Principles

In 1973 the US report Records, Computers and the Rights of Citizens led to the Fair Information Principles. The report states five requirements:

PrincipleWhat the 1973 report says
Transparency of collection“There must be no data record-keeping systems whose very existence is secret.”
Accessibility of personal info“There must be a way for an individual to find out what information about him is in a record and how it is used.”
Purpose limitation & consent“There must be a way for an individual to prevent information about him obtained for one purpose from being used or made available for other purposes without his consent.”
Correction of data“There must be a way for an individual to correct or amend a record of identifiable information about him.”
Safeguards & accountability“Any organization creating, maintaining, using or disseminating records of identifiable personal data must assure the reliability of the data for their intended use and must take reasonable precautions to prevent misuse of the data.”

05Privacy in the digital age

5.1 What changed

  • An explosion of data from social media, smart devices and the IoT.
  • Data monetization and surveillance capitalism: personal data is turned into money, mostly through targeted advertising.
  • The line between public and private data is blurred.
  • “The Internet never forgets.”
Threats by area
AreaMain privacy threats
Social mediaOverexposure, coerced consent, harassment, faithless friends
IoT devicesUnsecured sensors, constant tracking, data leaks
AI & facial recognitionMass surveillance, bias, deep fakes
Cloud servicesCross-border transfer, third-party processing
Mobile appsExcessive permissions, hidden data sharing

5.2 Threats in social media

ThreatMeaning
Manufactured disclosuresHow the site gets people to disclose more information, often with dark patterns (designs that trick users).
Extracting consentHow the site obtains consent, using psychological pressure: long unreadable terms, repeated requests, no real choice.
OverexposureNew features that make user data more visible and searchable.
Faithless friendsInformation shared by one of your “friends” with a wider audience.
Online harassmentInsults, bullying and threats online.

Social media is designed to be addictive, and this poses risks to the privacy of the individual.

5.3 Threats in the Internet of Things

IoT means interconnected devices that gather continuous data: smart watches, fridges, cars, security cameras.

  • Security issues: unencrypted data, remote hacking.
  • Privacy issues: profiling of behaviour and location.
  • What is needed: regulation and secure design.

5.4 Threats in AI and facial recognition

Facial recognition uses biometric data for authentication (unlocking a phone) or surveillance (cameras in public places). Its risks:

  • Misuse by authoritarian regimes;
  • bias against minorities (more recognition errors for some groups);
  • deep fakes and identity manipulation.
Question to think about“Should we use a technology just because we can?”

06Case studies

TikTok and youth data privacy (2021–2024)

Investigated in the EU and the US for collecting and processing minors’ personal data without adequate safeguards or parental consent.

Issues: children’s data protection; algorithmic profiling for targeted ads.

Outcome: multi-million euro fines under the GDPR; stricter parental control features required.

Aadhaar (India): biometric ID system

Collects fingerprints, iris scans and demographic data for over a billion citizens.

Issue: centralized storage of biometric data and its potential misuse.

Outcome: in 2018 the Indian Supreme Court declared privacy a fundamental right and restricted non-essential use of the data.

“Everything is watching you”

From the Joe Rogan Experience podcast with cybersecurity expert Ryan McBeth. Devices collect massive amounts of data: smart baby monitors, security cameras, doorbells; smart vacuums (Roomba, Xiaomi) that use lidar and cameras to map your home.

In 2022 MIT Technology Review showed that test images from Roomba’s AI mapping cameras, including people in private spaces, were leaked online. McBeth also showed how a normal-looking “smart” USB Type-C cable (the OMG cable) can exfiltrate data just by being plugged in.

Clearview AI and facial recognition

Scraped billions of facial images from public websites to build a database used by law enforcement, without consent.

Issues: violation of image ownership; unlawful biometric processing; ethical use of AI in law enforcement.

Outcome: banned or restricted in several countries for GDPR violations; sparked a global debate on facial recognition ethics.

Zoom’s “Zoombombing”

Zoom’s traffic was routed through China, and meetings were hijacked by outsiders (“Zoombombing”).

Issues: weak encryption and unclear data routing.

Outcome: the company introduced end-to-end encryption and transparency updates.

07Foundational principles of data privacy

The second part of the lecture starts with the seven foundational principles (the same as the principles of the GDPR, Art. 5).

  1. Lawfulness, fairness, transparency: data must be collected and processed legally and openly.
  2. Purpose limitation: use data only for the purpose it was collected for.
  3. Data minimization: collect the minimum amount necessary.
  4. Accuracy: keep data correct and up to date.
  5. Storage limitation: do not keep data longer than necessary.
  6. Integrity and confidentiality: protect data from unauthorized access or alteration.
  7. Accountability: organizations must demonstrate compliance with privacy rules.
ExampleA shop asks for your birth date, home address and ID number just to send you a newsletter. That breaks data minimization (an e-mail address is enough). If it later uses your address to sell it to an insurance company, it also breaks purpose limitation.

09Technical aspects of privacy protection

Ethics and law say what to protect. These techniques show how. Learn each one together with its example from the lecture.

TechniqueWhat it doesExample from the lecture
EncryptionEnd-to-end protection for communication and for data at rest.WhatsApp messages are end-to-end encrypted: only the sender and receiver can read them.
Anonymization & pseudonymizationRemoving or masking identifiers.The research team sees “Patient 1023” instead of “Jane Smith”.
Federated learningAI models are trained without sharing raw data.Google’s Gboard learns how you type; your typing stays on your phone, only model updates are shared.
Differential privacyAdds statistical noise to protect individual records.Apple Maps shows congestion from traffic data with random noise, so nobody can trace your route.
Zero-knowledge proofsCryptographic proof without exposing the underlying data.Logging in to a cryptocurrency exchange by proving you know your private key without sending the key.
Secure multiparty computation (SMPC)Data analysis across several organizations without revealing their private data.Several banks detect money-laundering patterns together without revealing their customer data.
TipAnonymization removes the link to the person for good. Pseudonymization replaces the name with a code (“Patient 1023”), so someone with the key could still link it back. The lecture groups them together as “removing or masking identifiers”.

10Individual rights under the GDPR and similar laws

RightWhat you can do
Right to accessSee what data is held about you.
Right to rectificationCorrect inaccurate data.
Right to erasure (“right to be forgotten”)Request deletion.
Right to restrict processingPause the use of your data.
Right to data portabilityReceive your data in a machine-readable format (to move it to another service).
Right to objectStop processing for certain purposes, e.g. marketing.
Right to non-automated decision-makingProtection from fully automated profiling and decisions.
Which right?
  • You want a copy of everything a gym app stores about you → access.
  • Your bank has the wrong address → rectification.
  • You want to move your playlists to another music service → portability.
  • You want no more marketing e-mails but keep the account → object.
  • A loan is refused by an algorithm with no human involved → non-automated decision-making.

11Privacy by Design: the modern standard

Privacy by Design was proposed by Ann Cavoukian, former Ontario Privacy Commissioner (Canada). The idea: build privacy into a system from the start, not add it after a problem. It has seven core principles:

  1. Proactive, not reactive: prevent problems before they happen.
  2. Privacy as the default setting: the user is protected without doing anything.
  3. Privacy embedded into design: part of the system, not an add-on.
  4. Full functionality: positive-sum, not zero-sum: privacy and functionality, not one against the other.
  5. End-to-end security: protection for the whole life cycle of the data.
  6. Visibility and transparency: the practices can be checked.
  7. Respect for user privacy: keep the system user-centric.
ExampleA new fitness app ships with location sharing switched off until the user turns it on. That is privacy as the default setting. Planning encryption before the first line of code is written is proactive, not reactive.

12Privacy laws and the EU GDPR

12.1 Privacy laws in general

Data collection laws focus on how data is collected, used and shared. Data protection includes the right to information self-determination. Three main areas of law affect privacy:

AreaProtects against
Media lawsIntrusion and defamation
Surveillance lawsSurveillance without a search warrant
Data protection lawsWrong handling of personal data

Countries have different laws. In general, a company is bound by the security and privacy commitments in its own policies, and companies are held accountable for security breaches.

12.2 The GDPR (Regulation 2016/679)

The General Data Protection Regulation is an EU regulation with strong data protection and user rights. Its goal is to give individuals control over their personal data.

  • Lawful, transparent, purpose-limited data use.
  • Rights to access, correct, erase and port data.
  • It also addresses data outside the EU.
  • It applies whenever personal data is processed and starts from the presumption that processing is illegitimate: the company must show that it is allowed.

12.3 Data controllers and accountability

  • Companies carry the burden of proof that processing is lawful.
  • They must show a legitimate basis and informed consent.
  • High-risk processing needs a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), and a Data Protection Officer (DPO) must be appointed.
  • Heavy fines for violations (cases against Meta, Amazon, Google).
  • Breaches must be reported within 72 hours (Art. 33).

12.4 What the GDPR means for developers

ArticleWhat to do in your system
Art. 5(1)(c)Collect only the data necessary for the purpose. Don’t store extra attributes “just in case”. (data minimization)
Art. 5(1)(b)Define why data is collected and use it only for that. Any new purpose requires new consent. (purpose limitation)
Art. 6(1)(a), Art. 7Clear, unambiguous consent forms. No pre-ticked boxes or forced consent (“Accept All”).
Art. 17Let users easily request full deletion, including backups when possible. (right to erasure)
Art. 5(2), Art. 30Keep records of processing, data sources and third-party sharing; be able to prove compliance at any time. (accountability)
Art. 33Report a personal data breach within 72 hours.

13The EU Digital Services Act (DSA, 2024)

The DSA has been part of European law since 2024 for all EU member states. Its goal is to protect the digital space against illegal content and to protect the fundamental rights of users.

  • It regulates digital platforms.
  • Illegal content must be removed quickly.
  • Minors are protected from targeted ads.
  • It increases transparency.
  • Stricter rules for large platforms (e.g. Meta, Google).
ArticleRequirement
Art. 25Avoid manipulative or deceptive interfaces that pressure users (dark patterns).
Art. 26Mark all ads clearly as “advertisements”.
Arts. 27–29Explain clearly how recommendations are generated.
Arts. 28–29Stronger privacy defaults, no profiling or ads based on sensitive data, accessible design.
Art. 42Platforms using automated curation or moderation publish annual transparency reports on algorithms, risk management and impacts.
GDPR vs DSAThe GDPR is about personal data: how it is collected and processed. The DSA is about online platforms: illegal content, ads, recommendations and manipulative design.

14Privacy laws around the world

The global trend: more and more countries copy the GDPR idea. The CCPA/CPRA (California) gives users rights to access, delete and opt out of data selling; LGPD (Brazil), PIPEDA (Canada) and PDPA (Singapore) are national equivalents; the EU AI Act focuses on risk and ethics in AI systems that process personal data.

15Best practices in IT and summary

15.1 Best practices in IT

  1. Collect minimal necessary data.
  2. Obtain explicit consent.
  3. Encrypt and secure data storage and transfer.
  4. Ensure transparency in data policies.
  5. Run regular privacy impact assessments.
  6. Promote user education and awareness.

15.2 A different view: the Missionary Church of Kopimism

Founded around 2010 by Isak Gerson, a philosophy student. Its members treat copying (Ctrl+C) and pasting (Ctrl+V) as holy symbols. Their main belief is that information should be free and shared, not controlled by governments or corporations. They do not worship a god; they see data and information as valuable and sacred. Sweden officially recognized it as a religion in 2012, though it is small and not traditional.

15.3 Summary

  • Privacy is “the right to be left alone” (1890); Prosser named four invasions (1960); today the Internet never forgets.
  • Privacy protects freedom, autonomy and identity; without it come chilling effects and surveillance.
  • Social media, IoT, facial recognition, cloud services and mobile apps bring new threats, as the case studies show.
  • Seven principles, ethical dimensions, technical tools, individual rights and Privacy by Design guide ethical data use.
  • Laws (GDPR, DSA, CCPA/CPRA, LGPD, PIPEDA, PDPA, AI Act) turn these ideas into duties, with fines for breaking them.

077 foundational principles

Big idea: collect little, for a clear reason, keep it right, safe and short, and be able to prove it.

  1. Lawfulness, fairness, transparency: legal and open.
  2. Purpose limitation: only for the original purpose.
  3. Data minimization: the minimum necessary.
  4. Accuracy: correct and up to date.
  5. Storage limitation: not longer than necessary.
  6. Integrity & confidentiality: no unauthorized access or change.
  7. Accountability: demonstrate compliance.
Trap: minimization is about how much data; storage limitation is about how long; purpose limitation is about what for.
A company keeps CVs of rejected applicants for 10 years. Which principle?

Storage limitation.

Which principle means “be able to prove you follow the rules”?

Accountability.

!Top exam traps

  1. 1890 Brandeis & Warren (“left alone”) vs 1960 Prosser (4 invasions).
  2. Contextual integrity is Nissenbaum, about the flow of information, not secrecy.
  3. Minimization (how much) vs storage limitation (how long) vs purpose limitation (what for).
  4. Restrict (pause) vs erasure (delete) vs object (stop for a purpose).
  5. Under the GDPR processing is presumed illegitimate until the company proves otherwise.
  6. Breach report: 72 hours, Art. 33.
  7. DSA = platforms (ads, recommendations, dark patterns, illegal content); GDPR = personal data.
  8. Differential privacy = noise; federated learning = data stays on device; ZKP = prove without revealing; SMPC = joint analysis.
  9. Privacy by Design is Cavoukian’s; “positive-sum” = privacy and functionality together.
  10. PIPEDA = Canada, PDPA = Singapore, LGPD = Brazil, CCPA = California.
